fluxprune: keeping the points that matter

fluxprune keeps a subset of a series' points, read as straight lines between them, for storage and processing rather than for compression: the first and last points (to stitch blocks), the minimum and maximum, then whichever points lower the time-weighted RMSE of the interpolation most. One O(n log n) pass ranks every point (bottom-up: repeatedly drop the point whose removal costs least, exact integrals per input segment); a rule picks how many to keep from the error curve that pass records; then 2 O(n) refinement passes move each kept point to where it fits best between its neighbours.

Data: 12 synthetic signals × 5 days of 1 s scans, cut into 1 h storage blocks (1,440 blocks of 3,600 points; tslab/prune/signals.py). Errors are relative to each block's range (max − min). Regenerate with uv run python make_prune_report.py in experimental/.

Error curves

What a rule sees: the relative RMSE of keeping k points (the ranking, before refinement), one hour of each signal. Smooth signals fall as k⁻² (sine), random walks as k⁻½ (temperature, pH), and white noise hardly at all until most points are kept; a step or ramp signal falls off a cliff and then sits on its noise. Sampling rate and block length

A rule that adapts to the data, not to how it was stored, should keep about as many points of an hour sampled every 10 s as of the same hour sampled every second (ratio 1 when the signal is captured at both rates; noise can't be), and about as many in one 12 h block as in twelve 1 h blocks (count and balance scale K with the block's hours). Relative to what?

Errors so far are relative to each block's range, so a block's precision follows its own range whatever the units or the offset: an hour of furnace that moves 4 °F around 2,115 °F gets the same relative precision as an hour of a valve that moves 0-100%. The alternatives are the block's largest absolute value, the geometric mean of the two, and a known span per series (here each signal's range over all its days, as a historian's per-tag span would give, but which a store that knows nothing of the series lacks). The ranking doesn't depend on the scale, only the rules' thresholds do. Six more signals where it matters:

The max_error pass

How max_error is met. Each line between the rmse pass's points that strays further than max_error is split from its left end. Every scan after an anchor bounds the slope of a line from the anchor that passes within max_error of it, from below and above. Swinging door's rule ends the line at the first scan its chord can't reach. Farthest reach, fluxprune's, scans on while the bounds still overlap (some line could still pass every scan so far), notes the last scan whose own chord satisfies them, and when they cross makes that scan the next anchor: with noise, a scan after one the chord can't reach is often reachable again. Fewest points is the exact minimum (dynamic programming over every feasible chord). Findings

  1. Ranking: bottom-up, then refine. Removing the cheapest point repeatedly (exact time-weighted L2, a heap, O(n log n)) gives a nested ranking of every point and the whole error curve in one pass, but greedy merging is myopic: about 30% above the optimum. Two O(n) passes that slide each kept point to its best spot between its neighbours bring it to 4-6% above the optimum on whole 1 h blocks at 12, 36 and 108 points (worst 1.4×), for +10-30% of the ranking's time. Top-down splitting (Douglas-Peucker's order, and that of perceptually important points) is better than plain bottom-up on step-like signals and much worse on others (up to 74× the optimum on the compressor). Visvalingam-Whyatt (bottom-up by triangle area) is 1.6-1.8× fluxprune's RMSE, and 1.03-1.07× with the same refinement passes: the passes do most of the work, and exact L2 is still the better order to start from. SWAB (Keogh et al. 2001) is bottom-up in a sliding window, for streams; on whole blocks it is plain bottom-up. At the same number of points, swinging door's RMSE is 1.3-1.5× fluxprune's, LTTB's 2-2.5×, and min/max buckets' and even spacing's 2-4×: the plotting downsamplers keep shape for the eye, not the interpolation.
  2. The error curve tells the signal apart from the noise. Smooth signals fall as k⁻², random walks as k⁻½, noise stays flat until most points are kept. The curve at k = n/2 measures the white noise: it is 0.29σ for white noise at every block size (tslab/prune/rules.py, noise), regular or irregular times.
  3. Fixed count spends the same on a valve that two points describe exactly as on white noise, and fixed tolerance is the opposite: calibrated to the same total it hands noise blocks everything (white noise 194 points/h against 36 average, a block up to 432) and its ε ends at 13% of the range for all the others: the worst mean error of all rules. Neither alone is a rate control for mixed data.
  4. The geometric mean of the two (balance, √(K·k_ε)) is a real compromise (slope ~0.6 against the tolerance's counts) and bounded by √(K·n), but noise is still the most "complex" thing it sees: white noise gets 4× the average. Raising ε to the block's own noise level (balance+noise) fixes that and reaches nearly the least mean RMSE possible for the total at 36/h (3.4% vs 3.3%) with the smallest largest block of the adaptive rules (132); at large budgets it stops early on purpose (it won't trace noise), so its mean levels off. Its weakness is the noise estimate: correlated noise (flow: τ 5 s at 1 s scans) partly reads as signal, so the count depends on the sampling rate there (×0.25).
  5. The marginal rules (stop when a point gains less than λ) give the least mean error for the total by construction (e + λk) but depend on the sampling rate: a noise point's gain scales as 1/n, so the same λ keeps 13-25× more noise points at 10 s than at 1 s. The user's prediction holds: on noise they either give up at once or never.
  6. The amortized rule (stop at the first k where the error left is under θ per point added) is the best one-knob rule here: within 4-21% of the least mean RMSE at every budget, largest block 2.5-3.7× the average, no noise estimate, and the most stable under a change of sampling rate (×0.80-0.98 for every signal). It stops where the error curve meets the line θ·k: in effect error and points traded at a fixed rate, so it grows with complexity sublinearly (k ∝ c^⅓ for a smooth signal's k⁻² curve, ∝ σ for noise). It is error_per_point in the API.
  7. Knee detection (stop when doubling the points stops paying) is unusable: periodic signals have plateaus at small k (12 h of a sine: 4 points) and random walks never stop (temperature: 3,061 of 3,600).
  8. Block length. Every rule but the fixed count keeps fewer points in one 12 h block than in twelve 1 h blocks, for two reasons. A parameter per block is spread over a longer block: θ / hours for the amortized rule (as K · hours for a count) takes its geometric mean over the 18 signals from 0.09 to 0.30 (θ per block: 0.01-0.48; θ / hours: 0.01-0.93, the low end the quiet level and the dropouts, whose range a single event sets). And a longer block has a wider range: with a known span per series instead, the same rule reaches 0.70. The rest is as it should be: twelve blocks keep twelve sets of required points, and one block can place its points where they matter most.
  9. Relative to what? Of the scales that need no knowledge of the series, the block's own range is the best: with the amortized rule at 36/h, the mean error over each signal's span is 1.3%, against 1.1% with known spans and 1.0% the least possible. It gives small variations on a large offset the precision they need by itself: the furnace (4 °F an hour around 2,115) and the grid frequency keep 27 and 57 points per hour, where known spans give 16 and 62. The largest |value| depends on where zero is: the same ambient temperature keeps 38 points per hour in °C and 5 in kelvin, and it starves exactly the signals that have little range for their offset (furnace and grid frequency: 4 per hour, at 2.8% and 9% of their span, against 0.34% and 2%). √(range · max|x|) halves both effects but keeps them (41 against 12 points for °C and K, grid 4% of span), and it helps block length less than a known span (0.43 against 0.70). The range has two costs: a quiet block makes its own dither look like signal (the quiet level keeps 74 points per hour tracing one 0.01 step, against 5 with its span), and a glitch that sets the range takes its block's precision (furnace with dropouts: 1.1% of span against 0.34%). A known span fixes both, and so does an absolute max_error guard (next).
  10. Swinging-door archives as input prune further, depending on what the archive kept: a tag whose CompDev is below its noise (flow, motor current: 1,300-3,000 archived points per hour) drops to 0.6-6% of the archive at the same settings, a smooth tag (temperature, pH: ~330/h) to 2-11%, and an archive that already has two points per hour stays as it is.
  11. max_error only adds points: the max_error pass splits each line between the rmse pass's points that strays further than max_error from the scans by farthest reach: from each anchor, the line to the farthest scan it can reach while every scan between stays within max_error. Swinging door's rule (the line ends at the first scan the chord can't reach) keeps 1.1-3× as many points on the noisy signals (vibration 170 against 66 per hour, batch 72 against 23, temperature 766 against 510), the exact minimum 4% fewer overall at about 34× the time (13 against 441 µs per block, up to 8.4 ms). At the same guarantee as swinging door (CompDev = max_error / 2, since at CompDev = max_error its line strays up to 1.6-2× that), fluxprune keeps fewer points on 12 of the 18 signals (batch 23 against 228 per hour, vibration 66 against 422, temperature 510 against 1,136, grid frequency 282 against 597) and as many on two; more only where the rmse pass already wanted more than the guard needs (the quiet level's dither: 77 against 2). A max_error below a signal's noise keeps most of the scans, as swinging door does (motor current: 3,160 per hour): at max_error = σ about half the scans, at 0.6σ two thirds, at 2.5σ an eighth, so prune() warns below σ, estimated from the error curve the ranking already has (no extra pass). max_points (here 360 per hour) caps that. The budget goes in a fixed order: the required points; then max_error, if it can be met within the cap, with the rmse pass taking what is left (its least significant points give way first, by a bisection on their number); and if it can't, max_error relaxed to about the tightest bound the cap allows (a bisection over the max_error pass), with no points from the rmse pass. The max_error pass always comes last, since adding a point can make the error worse. The cap bound only on the five noise-dominated signals; in their worst block the bound reached is 1.2× max_error for temperature, 1.3× for pH, 2.1× for white noise, 2.7× for flow and 11× for motor current, whose noise is five times max_error. Measured and not adopted: splitting the worst segment at its worst point when the cap binds (1.3-1.7× larger errors: it anchors on noise spikes); the max_error pass first and ranking again with its points fixed (the same RMSE at the same number of points, 3-5× slower); a secant search for the relaxed bound (9.4 max_error passes on average against 12.8, but up to 28 against 14); trying every number of the rmse pass's points instead of bisecting (0.4% lower RMSE on average).
  12. Refit (refit=True): least-squares values at the same kept times, the required points pinned to their samples, lower the RMSE by about a tenth overall, almost all of it on smooth or step-like signals (sine, compressor, batch: 0.5-0.8×) where a chord cuts a curve's corner; on noisy ones by 1-3%. It costs one O(n) tridiagonal solve. The values are then no longer readings, and it doesn't mix with max_error: a least-squares line through noise moves away from the scans that set the bound, so max_error still holds in only 41% of the blocks (none on the noisy signals, all on the smooth or quiet ones). It stays an option to measure on real data. Choosing the times and the values together (a free-knot fit, or Imai and Iri's 1986 fewest-segments algorithm for max_error with corners off the samples) could gain more and wasn't tried.

The API. prune(t, x) returns the kept times and values, the RMSE and largest error they leave, and rmse_met, max_error_met and max_points_met. The rmse pass is the default (error_per_point θ = 0.0015 keeps ~1% of 1 s scans in 1 h blocks on this corpus; the error relative to each block's range; θ / H for blocks of H hours keeps fewer per hour). Two optional guards bound it: max_points, a hard cap, and max_error, the largest error of any scan in value units, which also takes care of quiet blocks, glitches and the block length for a configured signal. θ = 0 with max_points keeps the best max_points; θ = ∞ with max_error runs the max_error pass alone. A run of non-finite values keeps its first point and the finite points around it (a forward hold), past max_points if need be. The other rules on this page (count, tolerance, balance, noise floor, marginal, knee) are experiments in tslab/prune/rules.py.

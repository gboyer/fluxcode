fluxcode timestamps: the time axis

By default, fluxcode stores each sample's timestamp exactly alongside its values (the last section covers optional rounding by a fraction of an interval). This report isolates the time axis (SPEC §3) and measures what timestamps add to a one-minute block group (60 blocks of 1,000 samples at a nominal 1 kHz, stored as int64 nanosecond ticks). Every figure below is the difference between encoding the block group with timestamps and encoding it without them. For reference, the values alone (a 4.12 Hz sine wave at default settings) take 17,987 bytes, 292 µs to encode, and 102 µs to decode.

Regenerate: cd experimental && uv run python make_time_report.py.

How timestamps are stored

  • Each block records a start timestamp, a step, and a reference. The step is the GCD of the block's intervals, and dividing each interval by that step produces a quotient written as the reference plus a residual. When all 999 intervals in a block are equal, every quotient matches the reference and the block is regular, needing no residual data (24 bytes before compression, and only a few bytes after). Any other block is irregular and stores its zigzagged residuals across 32 bit planes—or 64 in the rare long block where a residual reaches 232 or more (a multi-second gap in nanosecond ticks). Most planes are zero, and zstd strips them away.
  • The reference is chosen per block as either the rounded mean or the minimum. The mean works best for symmetric jitter around a center, while the minimum suits skewed distributions (such as gaps, events, or deadband logging) where the mean sits away from most intervals. Without a reference, intervals wobbling near 1,000 flip several bit planes at once, costing roughly a bit per sample.
  • A gap only affects its own block; the blocks before and after it remain regular.
  • Tick resolution is effectively free because the GCD factors out the grid, so a 1 ms grid costs the same whether stored in nanoseconds, microseconds, or milliseconds.
  • Block starts are delta-encoded from the previous block's start, so the 60 starts of a regular block group compress to just a few bytes.

The common shapes

In practice, most time series fall on either a perfect grid or a grid with occasional gaps. A noisy clock—such as host arrival timestamps—is the third common case, and the only one where timestamps take more space than the values themselves (about two and a half times as much here).

  • Perfect grid takes 62 bytes for 60,000 timestamps regardless of the start time, step size, or tick unit. Most of the decode time is simply writing out the 60,000 int64 ticks.
  • A few gaps turn only the affected blocks irregular. Because all but one interval in each affected block equals 1, its bit planes are almost entirely zero, adding roughly 20 to 60 bytes per gap (see the next section).
  • Noisy clock jitter is genuine entropy that no lossless coder can eliminate. At µs resolution, σ = 20 µs carries about 6.4 bits of entropy per sample, and the block group uses 7.1 bits; the extra overhead comes from encoding intervals (each the difference of two jittered times) with independent bit planes. When that jitter is measurement noise rather than signal, Params(time_error=0.1) lets fluxcode round it away, bringing the same clock down to 62 bytes (last section).

Rounding timestamps: Params.time_error

When timestamp jitter is just measurement noise, Params(time_error=e) allows fluxcode to round timestamps (e ranges from 0 to 0.5, with 0 keeping them exact). Each block gets a coarse, consistent step where at least 95% of timestamps stay within e of their own adjacent interval and no timestamp moves more than e of the block's mean interval. Sample values are left untouched and the wire format stays identical, so the decoder simply reads ordinary timestamps.

  • Validation rule. No timestamp may move by more than e mean intervals (the block span divided by its interval count, with 2% slack), and at most 1 in 20 may move by more than e times its own local interval (the shorter of the two intervals on either side). Only unusually close timestamps can exceed e relative to their local interval, and even then they remain bounded by the block mean.
  • Candidate search. The encoder tries candidates in order and takes the first one that passes. First, it checks the lattice continuing the previous block if that block was regular on the same clock. Next, it tries a 1-2-5 grid (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, … ticks), starting at the coarsest step up to 2e mean intervals (which clocks usually pass) or stepping down from a short sampled interval (the 3rd smallest of 15) until it passes. Each grid uses the clock's own phase—a multiple of one-tenth of the step, carried over from the previous block unless another fits clearly better—so free-running and drifting clocks snap to their own grids rather than the epoch's. Finally, for clocks whose period no 1-2-5 step divides (such as 1024 Hz or 60 Hz, where rounded intervals would alternate), it tries a custom lattice using the clock's typical interval as the period.
  • Smooth degradation. Once e reaches roughly 5× the clock's relative jitter (σ divided by the interval), a jittered clock snaps onto a regular grid and stores no residuals. Below that point, size drops in discrete steps as each finer 1-2-5 grid becomes eligible, so even a small tolerance still helps.
  • No-op on clean grids. Regular blocks are skipped entirely, and timestamps already on a grid are never altered.
  • Ordering and mutations. Timestamp order is preserved across blocks and block groups, and within time blocks every sample stays in its original block (a timestamp that would round past the end is clamped to the end). Rounding happens once during encoding: update_time_blocks stores newly added timestamps exactly, whereas replace_time_blocks re-encodes whole blocks from the supplied samples.

  • Jittered clocks drop from roughly 120 KB to 62–64 bytes once e reaches about 5σ (0.05 here), whether aligned to round times or free-running. When drift is also present, the phase tracks the clock block by block, reaching 79 bytes at 0.05 and 75 bytes at 0.1. Becoming regular speeds up encoding as well because regular blocks skip residual coding.
  • Non-1-2-5 clocks snap to their own custom lattices once e covers the jitter (around 5σ): 1024 Hz clock: 112,687 → 119 bytes at 0.05 and 93 at 0.1; 60 Hz clock: 143,935 → 160 bytes at 0.05 and 101 at 0.1.
  • Already-regular grids do not change, though verifying them has a small CPU cost: +43 µs (+15%) per block group on a perfect grid (checking order and regularity) and +23 µs (+8%) when there are a few gaps (attempting to round the irregular blocks).
  • The noisy clock (σ = 20 µs, or 2%) needs e = 0.1 to land cleanly on its grid; at 0.05, about 1% of its timestamps still fall onto a neighboring grid point.
  • Events and deadband logging have no underlying clock grid, though rounding still trims low-order bits (Poisson events shrink by 2.2× at 0.05). Because Poisson events contain many tight pairs and 95% of timestamps must stay within e of their local interval, they get a finer step than a periodic clock at the same e. Deadband timestamps already sit on a 1 ms grid mostly a few milliseconds apart, so any coarser step violates the local-interval bound for too many points; below e = 0.5 it saves nothing and costs +116 µs (+40%) per block group at 0.05 to check.

For the measurements behind these design choices (1-2-5 steps over powers of two, no value interpolation or resampling, and per-block phases), see docs/TUNING.md (Time error).

fluxcode on swinging-door historian data

Process historians that use swinging-door trending (SDT, as in PI and similar systems) scan each tag at a fixed rate, drop readings within an exception deadband (ExcDev) of the last reported value, and archive only the points that swinging door retains. The result is an irregular series of actual scans whose connecting straight lines stay close to every snapshot (nominally within CompDev, though up to about twice that in practice). This report measures fluxcode on those exported archives, encoding one block group per tag-day with exact timestamps. The test set covers six simulated tags (5 days each at 1 s scans), ranging from a valve that keeps 1 point per 2,000 scans to a noisy tag with a tight CompDev that keeps nearly every scan.

Always store the archived points themselves. Historians can also export a resampled regular grid (interpolated or forward-filled), but that discards the original points and takes more space—see resampled exports.

Regenerate: cd experimental && uv run python make_sdt_report.py (sizes and errors only, no timings). The simulator lives in tslab/common/historian.py.

Findings

  • The noise floor only activates when the historian kept nearly every scan. Because swinging-door points look like white noise without actually being noise, a block must have at least 90% one-scan intervals before the floor turns on. At each tag's nominal CompDev, no archive triggers it; even the densest tag (motor-current) has 80%–84% one-scan intervals, so all decimal tags decode identically to having the floor off. Across the CompDev sweep, the floor activates on motor-current at 0.25× its CompDev (94%–96% one scan): -46% in size, max error 0.6 of its own CompDevs; motor-current at 0.5× its CompDev (89%–92% one scan): -35% in size, max error 0.6 of its own CompDevs, where CompDev is set well below the noise (noise floor). At defaults, the float32 tag stays within 0.0008 CompDevs at 2.45 bytes per point.
  • Store archived points rather than resampled exports. Reading the same archive back on a 1 s grid uses 0.6–7.4× more bytes when interpolated and 0.6–2.3× more when forward-filled (resampled exports), and neither format preserves the original archived points.
  • Strong gains over lossless coders on dense tags. On dense tags, fluxcode is 1.8–2.3× smaller than columnar zstd and 1.7–6.4× smaller than Gorilla. Sparse tags (tank-level, valve, roughly 50 points/day) only take a few hundred bytes per day under any coder, so block overhead dominates: hourly blocks take 1.7–1.9× the space of one block per day (layout). Fixed time buckets keep storage management simple, and that overhead only affects tags that are already tiny.
  • Scan-aligned timestamps are cheap. Because every interval is a whole number of scans, timestamps add only 0.13–0.70 bytes per point on dense tags. Millisecond-resolution device timestamps add 1.11–1.32 bytes per point.
  • Negligible error added to SDT. At default settings, the trend reconstructed from fluxcode-decoded points has essentially the same worst-case error as the raw historian archive (accuracy), which is already around 2 CompDevs from swinging door itself.
  • SDT is not always smaller than storing every scan. At matched max error, encoding every 1 s scan directly with fluxcode beats SDT + fluxcode on flow, ph-float32, motor-current (SDT or every scan). On noisy tags, SDT retains most scans anyway while paying for irregular timestamps, whereas a regular grid's timestamps cost almost nothing. On smooth or flat tags, SDT still wins easily.

The tags

Each tag is scanned once per second for a day, then passed through an exception filter (ExcDev = CompDev / 2, the standard rule, also reporting the scan just before each exception) and swinging-door compression (CompMax = 8 h). Timestamps are whole seconds, matching a regular scan class, and errors are expressed in multiples of CompDev. Because swinging door archives the scan where its doors close, the resulting line is only guaranteed to stay within CompDev at that closing scan; earlier snapshots along the segment can stray by up to 2 CompDevs (about 1.9 here), and the exception filter pushes the total worst-case deviation to roughly 2.5 CompDevs.

Noise floor

fluxcode's noise floor (0.25σ by default) coarsens the quantization step on blocks whose samples look like white measurement noise. Sparse SDT points mimic white noise without actually being noise, because swinging door specifically retains points that a straight line cannot predict. To avoid misclassifying them, an irregular-time block only enables the noise floor if it has a regular cadence—at least 90% of its intervals equal to one scan (within 0.5× to 1.5× the median of 15 evenly spaced intervals)—and noise is then estimated strictly from consecutive scans. As a result, an archive that retained nearly every scan is treated like raw sensor data and gets the floor, while sparser archives keep their exact values. Resampled exports

Historians can also export data on a fixed 1 s grid, either linearly interpolated between archived points or forward-filled (held at the last archived value). fluxcode can compress those regular series (using defaults, 1,000-scan blocks, and 1 s timestamps), but storing the raw archived points is both smaller and more faithful. Interpolated values fall off the original decimal grid, and rounding along those ramps leaves small residuals. Figures are mean kB/day over the 5 days, with max |decoded − read-back| error in CompDevs.

SDT or every scan?

If raw scans are still available upstream of the historian, fluxcode can encode every scan directly instead of running SDT first.

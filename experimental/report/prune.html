fluxprune: keeping the points that matter

fluxprune selects a subset of points from a time series to be read back as piecewise-linear segments. It always keeps the first and last points (so adjacent blocks stitch cleanly) along with the minimum and maximum, then adds whichever points reduce the time-weighted RMSE most until another point no longer buys enough improvement. Calling prune(times, values) works out of the box with its defaults, and three parameters control what gets kept:

  • error_per_point (θ) sets the exchange rate between error and point count—choose it first by dividing the relative RMSE you would accept at some reference point count by that count. The default is 0.1% of the block range at a tenth of the input points.
  • max_points enforces a hard upper limit on the number of kept points.
  • max_error bounds the worst single-scan error in the signal's own units, adding points wherever needed.

The first two sections show how these settings behave across signals; the rest of the report walks through the algorithm and the experiments behind each design choice.

Dataset: 19 synthetic signals × 5 days of 1 s scans split into 1 h blocks of 3,600 points (tslab/prune/signals.py; the ranking and stopping-rule studies use the first 12 signals). Relative errors are normalized by each block's range (max − min). Regenerate with uv run python make_prune_report.py in experimental/.

Choosing θ and max_points

error_per_point (θ) acts as an exchange rate between error and point count. Prune adds points in order of how much they reduce the time-weighted RMSE and stops once an additional point no longer saves θ of that RMSE (measured relative to the block's range and above its white-noise floor; see the noise floor). Pick θ first by taking the RMSE you would accept at some reference point count and dividing by that count, so θ = Y / X reads as "Y error at X points." Note that X is not a fixed target: simpler blocks stop earlier, while busier blocks keep more points at a higher error. The default is 0.1% of the range at a tenth of the input length, θ = 0.001 / (n / 10), which is 2.8·10⁻⁶ for an hour of 1 s scans. Adding max_points puts a hard ceiling on top by dropping the least significant points from the RMSE pass. We compare three settings below:

  • default with no cap (equivalent to θ = 2.8·10⁻⁶ on these 1 h blocks);
  • default, max_points 360, capping the output at a tenth of the 1 s scans;
  • coarse, with θ = 0.0014 (half the range at 360 points, or 5% at 36 points).

Adding max_error

max_error puts an upper bound on how far any scan can deviate from the reconstructed line, in the signal's own units. It only adds points: after the RMSE pass finishes, any segment that strays beyond the bound is split (see the max_error pass). This catches features that RMSE averages away, such as narrow spikes, glitches, or quiet blocks whose range is just dither. Set it relative to the sensor's noise σ (estimated by Pruned.noise), because lines through noisy scans cannot stay within a few σ without retaining most of the data. On white noise at θ = 0.0014, max_error = 1σ keeps 1681, 2σ keeps 381, 3σ keeps 34, 5σ keeps 12 points per hour out of 3,600, and prune emits a warning if max_error is set below σ. When combined with max_points, the budget goes to max_error first and the RMSE pass uses whatever remains; if max_error cannot be met within the cap, it relaxes to roughly the tightest bound the cap permits (setting max_error_met to false). Below we test four settings on top of the coarse θ using per-signal tolerances (shown in the σ, loose, and fine columns):

  • coarse: θ = 0.0014 alone;
  • loose max_error: roughly the threshold an operator would notice;
  • fine max_error: around twice the sensor's noise, or the meaningful resolution for noise-free signals;
  • fine max_error, max_points 360: the same fine bound with a 360-point safety cap.

A fine bound around 2σ gets expensive on noisy signals, spending hundreds of points per hour mostly tracing noise (temperature averages 511, white noise 381, and the pyrometer's quiet hours jump from around ten points to over a hundred). The 360-point cap reins them in by relaxing the bound—in the worst block, from 1.05× the target (white-noise) up to 1.42× (grid-frequency). At 3σ or above, those same signals need far fewer points.

The noise floor

Straight lines through noisy samples always leave roughly σ of residual error until nearly every point is kept. On a quiet, noisy block whose range is only a few σ, that noise looks like real signal relative to the range, so the RMSE pass keeps spending points just to trace it. With the noise floor enabled (the default), the stopping criterion uses √(e² − σ²) instead of e—an estimate of the error against the true signal beneath the noise. σ is estimated in a single O(n) pass by taking each scan's distance from the chord connecting its two neighbors, normalizing by √(1 + w² + (1 − w)²) (where w is its relative time position between them), and scaling the median by 1.4826 (fluxprune/_noise.py). No smoothing is applied and the point ranking stays unchanged; only the stopping point moves. Archives

Any noise estimator assumes sample times are independent of sample values, which is false for historian archives where swinging door or a deadband retains points specifically because of their values. Even on a smooth tag, archived points pass white-noise tests just like raw scans and yield an apparent σ near CompDev. To prevent false triggers, the noise floor only runs when a block has a regular cadence—at least 90% of intervals equal to one scan, using fluxcode's fluxcode._noise.cadence check. Relative to what?

So far, errors have been normalized by each block's range, which keeps relative precision independent of units and DC offset: a furnace drifting 4 °F around 2,115 °F receives the same relative precision as a valve swinging from 0 to 100%. Alternatives include normalizing by the block's largest absolute value, the geometric mean of range and max|x|, or a known per-series span (modeled here as each signal's range across all days, similar to a historian's configured span—something a schema-free store does not have). The choice of scale does not affect the point ranking, only the stopping thresholds. The max_error pass

After the RMSE pass, any segment that strays beyond max_error is split starting from its left endpoint. Each subsequent scan constrains the upper and lower slope of a line from the current anchor that passes within max_error of that scan. Swinging door's rule terminates the segment at the first scan its chord cannot reach. Farthest reach (used by fluxprune) keeps scanning as long as the upper and lower slope bounds still overlap, tracking the farthest scan whose chord satisfies them; once the bounds cross, it makes that scan the next anchor. On noisy data, a later scan is often reachable even after an earlier one was not. Fewest points finds the exact minimum via dynamic programming over all feasible chords. Cells show points per hour and RMSE over the signal's span (starting from the same RMSE-pass points in every column), with per-block runtime for the max_error pass in the summary row.

Findings

  1. Bottom-up ranking plus refinement gets close to optimal. Repeatedly removing the cheapest point under exact time-weighted L2 using a heap (O(n log n)) produces a nested ranking and the full error curve in one pass, though greedy merging alone sits about 30% above the global optimum. Two O(n) refinement passes—sliding each kept point to the best position between its neighbors—close that gap to 4–6% above optimal on 1 h blocks at 12, 36, and 108 points (worst case 1.4×) while adding only 10–30% to ranking time. Top-down splitting (the Douglas-Peucker / PIP order) beats plain bottom-up on step-like signals but can blow up badly elsewhere (up to 74× optimal on the compressor). Visvalingam-Whyatt (bottom-up by triangle area) yields 1.6–1.8× fluxprune's RMSE on its own and 1.03–1.07× after the same refinement passes, confirming that refinement does most of the heavy lifting while exact L2 still provides a better starting order. SWAB (Keogh et al. 2001) runs bottom-up inside a sliding window for streaming, which reduces to plain bottom-up on full blocks. At matched point counts, swinging door has 1.3–1.5× fluxprune's RMSE, LTTB has 2–2.5×, and min/max buckets and uniform spacing have 2–4×, since plotting downsamplers preserve visual silhouette rather than linear interpolation accuracy.
  2. The error curve separates signal from noise. Smooth signals decay as k⁻², random walks decay as k⁻½, and white noise stays flat until nearly all points are kept. Evaluating the curve at k = n/2 gives a clean white-noise estimate of 0.29σ across all block sizes (noise in tslab/prune/rules.py), whether timestamps are regular or irregular.
  3. Neither fixed count nor fixed tolerance works for mixed data. Fixed count spends the same budget on a valve that needs two points as on pure white noise. Fixed tolerance fails in the opposite direction: calibrated to a 36/h average, it pours budget into noisy blocks (194 points/h on white noise, up to 432 in one block) and pushes ε up to 13% of range for everything else, yielding the worst mean error of any rule.
  4. Geometric-mean balancing helps, especially with a noise floor. Taking the geometric mean of fixed count and fixed tolerance (balance, √(K·k_ε)) produces a genuine compromise (scaling slope ~0.6 against tolerance, bounded by √(K·n)), but white noise still reads as the most "complex" signal and takes 4× the average budget. Raising ε to each block's noise level (balance+noise) fixes that, nearly matching the lowest possible mean RMSE at 36/h (3.4% vs 3.3%) with the smallest worst-case block among adaptive rules (132 points). At high budgets it intentionally levels off rather than tracing noise, though correlated noise (flow with τ = 5 s at 1 s scans) partially reads as signal and makes point counts sensitive to sampling rate (0.25× at 10 s).
  5. Marginal stopping rules break under resampling. Stopping when a point saves less than λ achieves the lowest possible mean error for a fixed total budget by construction (minimizing e + λk), but a noise point's marginal gain scales as 1/n. As a result, the same λ keeps 13–25× more noise points at 10 s sampling than at 1 s, and on noisy data marginal rules tend to either quit immediately or keep almost everything.
  6. The amortized rule is the best single-knob default. Stopping at the first k where remaining error per added point drops below θ (exposed as error_per_point) stays within 4–21% of the lowest possible mean RMSE at every budget without a noise floor, caps the largest block at 2.4–3.7× the average, and is the most stable across sampling rates (0.80–1.00× across all signals). Geometrically, it stops where the error curve crosses the line θ·k, trading error for points at a constant rate so counts grow sublinearly with complexity (k ∝ c^⅓ for smooth k⁻² curves, and k ∝ σ for noise).
  7. Adding the noise floor makes low θ practical. By evaluating the amortized rule on √(e² − σ²)—estimating σ in one O(n) pass from the median distance of each scan to its neighbors' chord—prune stops spending points on white noise. At θ = 0.0015 it slashes points on noisy blocks (white noise 95 → 11/h, motor current 67 → 12/h, quiet level 77 → 19/h, vibration 47 → 18/h) while leaving smooth and quantized signals unchanged, at a cost of 0–10% higher RMSE against the noisy scans. Calibrated to 36/h it matches the optimal mean RMSE (3.3%, tying marginal (RMSE)) with half the median error (0.95% vs 1.7%, compared to 3.6% mean without the floor), and at 324/h it levels off at 2.8% vs 1.9% rather than chasing noise. Lowering θ no longer wastes points on noise: at θ = 1.5·10⁻⁶, white noise stays at 17/h instead of 3,142/h while smooth signals keep refining. On the pyrometer, the floor trims quiet hours from 94 to 11 points at θ = 0.0015 (improving true-curve RMSE from 0.66 °C to 0.56 °C), while narrow spikes are preserved by a tighter θ (default: 236 points/busy hour, 22/quiet hour, every peak within 7 °C) or max_error = 10σ (162 points, same peaks). At the default θ, the floor is essential: without it, noisy signals keep most of their scans (white noise 2,941/h, motor current 2,705/h, pyrometer quiet hours 2,933/h) versus 18, 22, and 22 with it. There are two trade-offs: correlated noise still looks partly like signal at fast scan rates (making flow drop to 0.14× at 10 s scans, and temperature/pH to 0.66–0.69×), and on pure white noise the few remaining lines anchored at the required min and max lean slightly further (worst of 120 hours is 0.48σ from zero vs 0.38σ without the floor, though the mean improves from 0.33σ to 0.26σ). Because value-dependent archive timestamps violate the white-noise assumption—causing SDT archives to look like white noise with σ ≈ CompDev (3× raw noise on temperature) and doubling scan RMSE from 1.9% to 3.3%—the floor only activates when at least 90% of intervals match the scan cadence (using fluxcode's cadence test), leaving SDT archives untouched. Scoring against a smoothed copy of the scans was also tested and rejected because chords between noisy samples are no closer to a smooth curve, requiring a tuning window and shaving narrow peaks.
  8. Knee detection is not viable. Stopping when doubling the point count stops paying off fails on both ends: periodic signals hit early plateaus (4 points for 12 h of a sine wave), while random walks never stop (3,061 of 3,600 points on temperature).
  9. Longer blocks keep fewer points per hour. Every rule except fixed count uses fewer points in one 12 h block than in twelve 1 h blocks. With θ / hours for the amortized rule (analogous to K · hours for count), the geometric-mean ratio across the 18 signals is 0.30 (ranging from 0.03 to 0.90, lowest on quiet-level/dropout signals where one event sets the range and on white noise where the floor holds counts roughly constant). Wider block ranges account for part of the drop—using a known per-series span raises the ratio to 0.55—and the rest is expected, since twelve blocks store twelve sets of required endpoints/extrema while one long block allocates points where they matter most.
  10. Block range is the best schema-free error scale. Calibrated to 36/h with the amortized rule, normalizing by block range yields 1.2% mean RMSE over signal span, close to the 1.0% achieved with known spans (and the 1.0% theoretical minimum). It automatically gives small variations on large offsets appropriate precision: furnace (4 °F/h around 2,115 °F) and grid frequency keep 29 and 100 points/h, close to 23 and 126 with known spans. Normalizing by max|x| depends arbitrarily on where zero sits (ambient temperature keeps 74 points/h in °C vs 14 in kelvin) and starves high-offset signals (furnace and grid frequency drop to 9 and 8 points/h, pushing error to 1.2% and 4.7% of span vs 0.32% and 1.5%). Using √(range · max|x|) cuts both distortions in half without eliminating them (64 vs 26 points for °C vs K, grid frequency 2.7% of span) and improves block-length scaling less than a known span (0.40 vs 0.55). Block range has two weaknesses—quiet blocks treat dither as signal (37 points/h tracing a 0.01 step on level-quiet vs 6 with known span, or 77 without the noise floor) and glitches widen the range and coarsen precision (furnace-dropout rises to 1.1% of span vs 0.32%)—both of which are solved by a known span or an absolute max_error guard.
  11. Swinging-door archives prune further depending on noise. Tags whose CompDev sits below their noise floor (flow and motor current, archiving 1,300–3,000 points/h) shrink to 0.6–6% of the archive at the same settings; smooth tags (temperature and pH at ~330/h) shrink to 2–11%; and already-sparse archives (~2 points/h) pass through unchanged.
  12. max_error and max_points outperform swinging door. The max_error pass splits any segment exceeding the tolerance using farthest reach: extending from each anchor to the farthest scan whose chord keeps every intermediate scan within max_error. Standard swinging door (stopping at the first unreachable scan) keeps 1.1–5× as many points on noisy signals (vibration 176 vs 36/h, batch 73 vs 23/h, temperature 766 vs 509/h), while exact dynamic programming saves only 4% more points at roughly 38× the runtime (490 µs vs 13 µs per block, up to 8.4 ms). At the same worst-case guarantee as swinging door (CompDev = max_error / 2, since CompDev = max_error allows 1.6–2× excursions), fluxprune uses fewer points on 12 of 18 signals (batch 23 vs 228/h, vibration 36 vs 422/h, temperature 509 vs 1,136/h, grid frequency 282 vs 597/h), ties on two, and uses more only where the RMSE pass already wanted extra points (19 vs 2 on level-quiet's dither). Setting max_error below the noise level forces any method to keep most scans (3,160/h on motor current; roughly half at σ, two-thirds at 0.6σ, and one-eighth at 2.5σ), which is why prune() warns below σ. Adding max_points (360/h here) allocates points in strict priority: required points first, then max_error if feasible within the cap (with the RMSE pass taking any leftover budget via bisection on its least significant points), or else relaxing max_error via bisection to the tightest bound the cap allows with zero RMSE-pass points. The max_error pass always runs last because inserting a point can increase error elsewhere. The cap only bound on the five noise-dominated signals, relaxing the worst block to 1.2× max_error on temperature, 1.3× on pH, 2.1× on white noise, 2.7× on flow, and 11× on motor current (where σ is 5× max_error). Alternatives tested and rejected: splitting the worst segment at its worst point under a cap (1.3–1.7× higher error from anchoring on noise spikes), running max_error before ranking with its points pinned (same RMSE at equal count, 3–5× slower), secant search for the relaxed bound (9.4 passes mean vs 12.8, but up to 28 vs 14 worst-case), and sweeping every RMSE-pass count instead of bisecting (only 0.4% lower mean RMSE).
  13. Refit (refit=True) helps smooth signals but breaks max_error. Solving an O(n) tridiagonal least-squares fit for interior values (keeping required points pinned) cuts RMSE by roughly 10% overall—most of it on smooth or step-like signals (sine, compressor, batch at 0.5–0.8×) where chords clip corners, and only 1–3% on noisy signals. Because refit values are no longer original readings and least-squares lines shift away from scans that defined the tolerance, max_error continues to hold in only 41% of blocks (0% on noisy signals, 100% on smooth or quiet ones). It remains an optional flag for evaluation on real data; joint time-and-value optimization (such as free-knot splines or Imai–Iri 1986 fewest-segments) could improve further and was not tested.

API summary. prune(t, x) returns the kept timestamps and values, the resulting RMSE and max error, and the status flags rmse_met, max_error_met, and max_points_met. By default it runs the RMSE pass with noise_floor=True and error_per_point (θ) set to 0.1% of block range at a tenth of the input length, θ = 0.001 / (n / 10) (2.8·10⁻⁶ for 1 h of 1 s scans, keeping ~4% of scans on average and 2% on the median block). Scaling θ by input length keeps density consistent across longer blocks, though a slower sampling rate keeps fewer points per hour. Two optional guards layer on top: max_points sets a hard ceiling, and max_error caps single-scan error in signal units (handling quiet blocks, glitches, and long blocks for configured tags). Setting θ = 0 with max_points keeps the best max_points points, while θ = ∞ with max_error runs only the max_error pass. Runs of non-finite values always retain their first point and the adjacent finite points (forward hold), even past max_points if necessary. The other stopping rules evaluated on this page (count, tolerance, balance, noise-raised ε, marginal, and knee) are experimental baselines in tslab/prune/rules.py.
